# Research Basis: Deceptive Design

Use these sources to structure a review and support carefully scoped statements. They do not replace jurisdiction-specific legal analysis.

## European Data Protection Board

- [Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns in social media platform interfaces, final version 2.0 (2023)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-032022-on-deceptive-design-patterns-in-social-media-platform_en)
- The guidance groups patterns affecting personal-data choices under **overloading**, **skipping**, **stirring**, and **obstructing**. Examples include continuous prompting, privacy mazes, too many options, visual/emotional steering, dead ends, excessive steps, and misleading actions.
- Scope: social-media interfaces and GDPR data-protection context. Do not present its list as exhaustive or as an all-product legal test.

## U.S. Federal Trade Commission

- [Bringing Dark Patterns to Light (FTC staff report, 2022)](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P214800%20Dark%20Patterns%20Report%209.14.2022%20-%20FINAL.pdf)
- The report discusses deceptive endorsements/ads, difficult cancellation, hidden terms or fees, and interfaces that obscure or subvert privacy choices. It gives examples such as asymmetric consent choices, confusing toggles, repeated prompts, and data-maximizing defaults.
- Scope: FTC staff report and U.S. consumer-protection discussion. It is a source for risks and examples, not a blanket legal ruling.

## European Commission

- [Digital Services Act overview](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/digital-services-act)
- The Commission describes a DSA ban on dark patterns for online platforms, with examples including aggressive pop-ups and confusing or misleading consent buttons. Applicability depends on the service, actor, provision, and facts; check the current law and guidance before compliance advice.

## Audit translation

Across these sources, inspect the whole task and compare the practical burden of choosing, declining, changing, and leaving. Record the observed sequence and user effect. Use labels such as `observed pattern`, `potential risk`, and `needs legal review` to preserve the difference between evidence and interpretation.
